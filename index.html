Ethics Unbound
A Contemporary Guide to the Good Life
▼
What is the difference between Ethics and Morals?
EthicsMorals
The branch of philosophy that studies what is right or wrong — a systematic, reasoned inquiry.Personal principles and values about right and wrong, often rooted in culture, religion, or upbringing.
More theoretical; it asks "What is good?" at a universal level.More practical; it reflects what a person actually believes and does.
Can be applied universally across different groups.May vary from person to person or culture to culture.
Key Distinction: Ethics is the study; morality is the lived practice. A person can have strong personal morals yet still engage in unethical behavior (e.g., hypocrisy). As illustrated by the song "Banal na Aso, Santong Kabayo" — external religiosity without moral conversion is empty faith.
Why should one act ethically?

1. It's a requirement for life.

It's our biological imperative as humans to survive and thrive, and ethics are part of the complicated structure of humanity that helps us determine the best ways to act so that each of us may live a long, productive life.

2. It's a requirement for society.

To be a member of society in good standing, one must follow the codes and laws that govern that culture.

3. For religious purposes.

People who subscribe to this type of ethics act in accordance with the rules set forth by an organized religion, and those rules are derived from sacred texts or the direction of a divine entity.

4. For self-interest.

Some ethicists believe that humans act out of self-service, that they do things with their own interests in mind.

5. Because humans are naturally ethical.

Are humans naturally ethical? Certain acts are just naturally good or naturally bad — humans pursue the moral life because of an innate sense of right.
Why care about Ethics?
  • Fosters positive relationships and trust between people
  • Promotes fairness and social harmony
  • Creates a more just and equitable society
  • Encourages responsible decision-making
  • Enables us to navigate complex moral dilemmas
James 2:26: "For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead." — Being "religious" is not the same as being moral. Faith must be expressed through ethical action.
What are the Core Principles of Ethical Thinking and their role in moral decision-making?
1

Autonomy / Respect for Persons

Acknowledge a person's right to make choices, to hold views, and to take actions based on personal values and beliefs — e.g., obtaining informed consent before a medical procedure.

2

Beneficence

Striving to do good that benefits others. Provide benefits to persons and contribute to their welfare — actions done for the benefit of others. E.g., a doctor provides free medical treatment to underserved communities.

3

Nonmaleficence

Obligation not to inflict harm intentionally. In medical ethics, the physician's guiding maxim is "First, do no harm."

4

Justice

Treat others equitably, distribute benefits/burdens fairly. E.g., a school offers scholarships to students from low-income families to ensure equal opportunities for education.

5

Veracity

Telling the truth and being honest. Moral decisions require honest communication, even when the truth is difficult.

These four principles (Respect for persons/autonomy, Beneficence, Nonmaleficence, Justice) were identified by Tom Beauchamp and Jim Childress as forming a commonly held set of pillars for moral life.
What is a Moral Standard? What are Normative Standards and other Normative Systems?

Moral Standard = A code of what is right and wrong. It is the foundation of ethical judgment — the measure by which actions are evaluated as good or evil.

Normative Systems = Prescriptive frameworks that guide and direct human behavior. They tell us what we ought to do.

Etiquette

Social rules for polite behavior — manners, social norms, cultural variations. Not legally enforceable.

Law

Enforceable rules of conduct with legal regulations, rewards, and penalties. Created by human authorities.

Religion

A set of beliefs and practices with moral codes based on spiritual beliefs or deity — e.g., the Ten Commandments.

Ethics / Morality

The broadest normative system — concerned with universal principles of right and wrong that transcend culture.
What are the Challenges of Ethics in Real-Life Situations?
⚡ Moral Relativism
The view that ethical standards are not universal but relative to individual beliefs, cultures, or societies. This leads to ethical ambiguity and undermines the idea of universal moral principles. Challenge: If everything is relative, how can we condemn any act as truly wrong?
🌍 Cultural Differences
Different cultures have diverse ethical frameworks shaped by historical, social, and religious factors. Cultural diversity enriches our understanding of ethics but can lead to dilemmas when values clash — e.g., practices that one culture celebrates may violate another's moral standards.
⚖️ Conflicting Values
In complex situations, individuals may face conflicting values or obligations — e.g., loyalty vs. honesty, individual freedom vs. community welfare, following orders vs. moral conscience.

The Starting Point of Morality
God, Moral Theology, and Images of Divine Love
▼
What does it mean to say that God is the starting point of morality?

Moral Theology is the study of revelation as the rule of human behavior so that humanity can attain salvation. It properly begins with God and God's love.

St. John Paul II: "The moral life is a response to the many gratuitous initiatives taken by God out of God's love." — Moral life begins with God's initiative, not our own.

The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that God freely created human persons to share in His blessed life. Since God created humanity, humans cannot be the reason and purpose of themselves. Therefore, the focus of Moral Theology is "the final end of man created in the image of God: beatitude (happiness in God), and the ways of reaching it."

Key Distinction — Moral Theology vs. Ethics

  • Moral Theology is also called Christian Ethics or Christian Morality
  • It focuses on identifying human acts as good or evil
  • Ultimately, it teaches how to discern good and avoid evil
  • It studies "man's way to God in the light of what God has revealed about man"
Why is morality without God considered incomplete in Christian ethics?

In Christian ethics, morality is fundamentally a response to God's love. Without God as the starting point, morality loses its ultimate foundation, purpose, and source. Moral life is not merely about rules — it is about relationship with God.

  • Without God, there is no ultimate telos (end goal) — no beatitude to strive toward
  • Morality becomes purely human-centered, subjective, or culturally relative
  • The Trinity — as a communion of love — is the model for all moral relationships. Without God, this model disappears
  • As Andrei Rublev's Trinity icon shows: God invites us into a community of love. Moral life is our response to that invitation
How does our image of God influence our moral decisions?

Since moral life is a response to God, how we imagine God directly shapes how we respond morally. A distorted image of God produces a distorted morality.

If our image of God becomes less loving and less merciful, our treatment of ourselves and others also becomes less loving and less merciful.

For example:

  • If we see God as a harsh judge → morality becomes fear-driven and legalistic
  • If we see God as a loving Father → morality becomes a joyful response to love
  • If we see God as distant → we separate our spiritual life from our ethical life
What are the Images of God in the Gospels? Analyze each.
1. The Father in the Parable of the Prodigal Son (Lk 15:11-32)

A younger son squanders his inheritance on reckless living, then returns in repentance. The father runs to meet him, embraces him, and throws a feast.

Analysis: God's love is unconditional — it does not depend on our behavior. Our sins do not diminish God's love; our good works do not increase it. God does not force us — He invites us. The elder son who sought to "earn" the father's love represents a distorted, transactional view of God.

Moral Implication: We are called to love freely, not as a transaction. Moral life is a response of gratitude, not payment of a debt.

2. The Good Shepherd (Jn 10:11-18; Lk 15:4-7)

A shepherd leaves the 99 sheep to find the one that is lost. When found, he carries it home rejoicing.

Analysis: God does not desire that anyone would be lost to sin. God has compassion for sinners and will actively seek us out. This image reveals God's personal, persistent pursuit of each individual soul.

Moral Implication: We should never give up on ourselves or others. No one is too lost for God to find.

3. The Suffering God (Jn 3:16; the Crucifixion)

For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life.

— John 3:16

Analysis: God's love is so profound that He was willing to suffer for us. The cross is the ultimate expression of self-giving love — God who gives Himself totally does not let rules get in the way of loving. God's response to sin is forgiveness, not punishment.

Moral Implication: The Crucified Christ is the perfect image of God. When we doubt God's love, we look to the cross.

What are the Distorted Images of God? Explain each.

Source: Kevin O'Neil & Peter Black, The Essential Moral Handbook (Ligouri, 2003). Distorted images arise from imperfect human experiences (rejection, abuse, betrayal) and misinterpreted Scripture passages.

✗ 1. God as Distant from the Concerns of the World ("God Who Is Only for Myself")
A personalized God who has no influence or concern about how I live my moral life. This leads to a separation between spiritual life and moral life — "I can say I love God but still hate my neighbor."
Correction: God is concerned about the world and about how we live. An authentic relationship with God informs and shapes our activity in the world. Our prayer life should affect how we relate to our neighbor.
✗ 2. God as Strict Lawgiver
Overemphasis on God as lawgiver encourages a moral life dominated by law and external obedience. It creates a morality overly concerned with external behavior with little attention to growth in personal character — morality becomes a checklist.
Correction: CCC 1691 — the goal of moral life is the imitation of Christ, not merely observation of laws. God loves us first and invites us to respond. Moral laws guide our love response, not replace it.
✗ 3. God as a Judge Who Rewards and Punishes
A God who watches our every action and punishes evil/rewards good. This inspires fear in the moral life — one seeks to be good only to avoid punishment.
Correction: God is predominantly a God of mercy. God does not punish — if we choose to remain apart from God, we condemn ourselves. Christ on the cross shows that God's response to sin is forgiveness.
✗ 4. God Who Tests Our Faith
The idea that God sends suffering, trials, and hardships as "tests" of faith. This can lead to passive acceptance of evil or the belief that God is indifferent to our pain.
Correction: A God who has an eternal love for us is not interested in testing us — He already loved us from the beginning of creation. Suffering is not punishment or a test designed by God.
✗ 5. God Who Controls My Life
The belief that God is a puppeteer who controls every detail of our lives, removing human freedom and responsibility. This leads to moral passivity — "whatever happens is God's will."
Correction: God respects human freedom. We respond freely to God's love. Moral life requires our active, free participation — not fatalistic surrender.

Sources of Christian Morality
Scripture · Natural Law · Magisterium
▼
Why must Scripture exercise authority on the moral life of Christians?

The Bible is inspired by God and written down by human authors. As the revealed Word of God, it is authoritative in revealing moral positions and the ethical values promulgated by God.

  • The Bible has always been the starting point of Christian morality — it reveals Christ, whom we should imitate
  • The primary concern of the Bible in morality is to exhort people to be and do more than the minimum
  • Scripture reveals the life of Jesus as the main source for learning how to live a Christian life
  • The moral doctrine Jesus preached — his words, deeds, and precepts — constitutes the "moral rule of the Christian life"

Therefore, be imitators of God, as beloved children, and live in love as Christ loved us.

— Ephesians 5:1-2 (New Testament exhortation to holiness)
Rules of morality derived from Scripture must be grounded in the tradition of the community and in harmony with reason and human nature.
The Critical Approaches of Sacred Scripture to Moral Theology — Why read the Bible in the context of faith?

The Bible took more than a thousand years to take shape, written in different periods, social settings, and literary genres. It does not have one theology — it has theologies. We cannot impose a single interpretation on its ethical content.

Four Critical Tasks:

1. Exegetical Task

Determine the original meaning of the text in its historical context. Ask: Who wrote it? To whom? Why? What does it teach today?

2. Hermeneutical Task

Determine the meaning of the text today. "What does this text mean for us now?" It moves from ancient words to contemporary life.

3. Methodological Task

Determine how to use Scripture for moral reflection. Allow the Bible to prescriptively guide our moral judgments — e.g., Commandments, Beatitudes.

4. Theological Task

Determine ways to combine the Bible with other moral wisdom sources — natural law, experience, church tradition, the Magisterium.
Warning: We must be careful not to use Bible passages out of context. Example: "An eye for an eye" (Ex 21:24) was originally intended to limit excessive punishment, not justify revenge. Always ask: Is my interpretation consistent with the love of Christ?
Why is Natural Law considered universal? How does it help people distinguish good from evil?

Natural Law, as developed by St. Thomas Aquinas (13th century, influenced by Greek philosophy — Socrates, Plato, Aristotle), is considered universal because it is written in human nature itself, accessible to all people through reason — regardless of religion or culture.

Three Types of Law (St. Thomas Aquinas)

  • Eternal Law (lex eterna) — God's immutable divine wisdom governing all creation
  • Natural Law (lex naturalis) — Part of the eternal law revealed by reason; written in human nature ("do good, avoid evil")
  • Human Law (lex humana) — Laws created by human authorities to govern society

Three Human Inclinations that ground Natural Law:

1

Self-Preservation (Commonality with All Beings)

The inclination to preserve life. Thus, killing is wrong — it violates this basic inclination shared with all living beings.

2

Reproduction (Commonality with Animals)

The inclination to propagate the species and care for offspring. Thus, acts that violate the integrity of family and procreation are considered disordered.

3

Reason and Social Harmony (Uniquely Human)

The inclination toward knowledge, truth, and social harmony. Humans are "social beings" (Aquinas) — not meant to live in isolation. Our choices affect others.

What is the relationship between Natural Law and the Bible?

Natural Law and Scripture are complementary, not contradictory. They both point to the same moral truth:

  • Scripture = revealed moral truth (from divine revelation)
  • Natural Law = moral truth discoverable through human reason (from God's creation)
  • Both have the same ultimate author: God
  • Example: "You shall not kill" (Scripture, Ex 20:13) aligns with the Natural Law inclination toward self-preservation
The moral rules from Scripture must be grounded in tradition and in harmony with reason and human nature — showing that Natural Law and Scripture work together.
What is the role of the Magisterium? Who exercises it? Why is it important?

The Magisterium is the Church's official teaching authority — the living, authentic interpreter of God's Word (both Scripture and Tradition).

Who Exercises It?

The Pope and the Bishops in communion with him — the successors of the Apostles.

What Does It Do?

Interprets Scripture and Tradition, defines dogmas, and provides moral guidance on contemporary issues.

Why Important?

Ensures unity of faith, guards against misinterpretation, and applies timeless principles to new moral situations.
The Magisterium is one of the five sources of Christian morality: Jesus → Bible → Magisterium → Natural Law → Experience.

Extra Fact: Two Forms of the Magisterium

  • Ordinary Magisterium: Day-to-day teaching through encyclicals, pastoral letters, catechism
  • Extraordinary (Solemn) Magisterium: Infallible definitions — ex cathedra statements by the Pope or Ecumenical Councils

Human Freedom
Authentic Freedom · Fundamental Option · Moral Choice
▼
What is true freedom vs. what freedom is NOT?
Freedom IS NOT…Freedom IS…
The right to do or say anything

Freedom is the power, rooted in reason and will, to act or not to act, to do this or that, and so to perform deliberate actions on one's own responsibility.

— CCC 1741
A license to do whatever we want
The absence of rules
An emotional reaction
Freedom is an intrinsic and essential property of the human person. The ability to make choices and perform actions sets humans apart from other beings. Two elements define freedom: Voluntariness (acting out of one's own free will) and Responsibility (being accountable for one's actions and consequences).
What is Authentic Christian Freedom? Analyze "Freedom From" and "Freedom For."

The Gospel of Jesus Christ is a message of liberation and freedom. Authentic freedom is not found in prejudice, deceit, or ignorance — but in seeking the truth that leads toward the Good.

"Freedom FROM"

Liberation from obstacles that prevent us from acting freely:
  • Interior: ignorance, disordered passions, fears, bad habits, prejudice, psychological disturbances
  • Exterior: violent forces or threats of violence
  • Biological: inherited defects, handicaps, drugs
  • Psychological: interior, unconscious compulsions

"Freedom FOR"

Freedom directed toward positive goals — it is both a process and a task:
  • We strive to overcome obstacles and sin (our task)
  • We strive to responsibly grow (our process)
  • Directed toward: Freedom, Holiness, Fullness of Life in Christ
Explain Fundamental Option and Fundamental Choice.
Fundamental OptionFreedom of Choice

A life orientation — the direction of one's whole life either toward or away from God.

  • Positive: A life of grace, directed toward God — a living acceptance of God
  • Negative: Sin — a life directed away from God
  • It is a response to the innate human desire for God at our deepest core
  • Question: "What kind of person am I choosing to become?"

The freedom to choose specific acts — to act this way or that way, to do good or evil.

  • Involves awareness of options
  • Involves deliberation (thinking)
  • Involves a voluntary decision
  • Question: "What will I choose to do?"
The Fundamental Option is the deeper level of freedom — it shapes the meaning and weight of our individual choices. Repeated choices either strengthen or weaken our fundamental orientation toward God.
"The more one does what is good, the freer one becomes." How does this apply to real-life moral decisions?

This is a central principle of Christian moral life: virtue increases freedom. When we consistently choose the good, we develop habits (virtues) that make it easier and more natural to continue choosing the good. Sin, on the other hand, is enslaving — it creates disordered attachments that limit freedom.

  • Real-life application: A person who consistently practices honesty finds it easier to be honest even in difficult situations — they are freer from the temptation to lie
  • A person enslaved to addiction has severely diminished freedom — their choices are constrained by the addiction
  • Freedom grows through virtue: patience, courage, justice, prudence
Elements determining the morality of an act: (1) Act — what is done, (2) Purpose/Intention — why it is done, (3) Circumstances — the context. All three must be good for an act to be morally good.

Principles in Judging Morality

  • If the act, purpose, AND circumstances are all good → the act is morally good
  • If the act is intrinsically evil → never allowable, regardless of purpose or circumstances
  • If the act is good or neutral → morality is judged by purpose and circumstances
  • Circumstances may mitigate, create, or aggravate sin
What limits or shapes human freedom?
  • Ignorance — lack of knowledge can diminish moral responsibility
  • Passion and emotion — strong feelings can cloud judgment
  • Fear and duress — acting under threat reduces voluntary choice
  • Bad habits (vice) — repeated sin weakens the will's capacity to choose the good
  • Psychological disorders — unconscious compulsions can reduce freedom
  • Social and structural sin — unjust social structures constrain individual freedom
  • Grace — paradoxically, grace expands freedom by healing the will and directing it toward the Good

The Human Person
Christian Anthropology · Bioethics · Moral Principles
▼
What is the Christian Understanding of the Human Person?
St. Francis de Sales: "Man is an epitome of the world; he is a little world himself." — The human person contains within themselves all the tensions and beauty of the universe.

Christian morality holds that the human person is the key to moral theology. Moral life is the call to become loving persons — life-with-others-in-community before God, in imitation of Jesus Christ.

Related Terminology

  • Man — general term for the entire human race
  • Human — refers to humans as a species (Homo Sapiens)
  • Human Being — distinguishes humans from other animals
  • Person — a human being granted recognition of rights, protection, responsibilities, and dignity
  • Personhood — the state of being a person
  • Human Nature — characteristics that distinguish humans from all other creatures
What is Dualism? Why is it wrong?

Dualism is the philosophical view (rooted in Greek philosophy) that the human person is composed of two distinct and separate substances — the body (material) and the soul (spiritual) — that are opposed to each other.

In dualism: Body = prison, machine, instrument, inferior. Soul = superior, immortal, the "real self."

Why it's wrong:

  • It degrades the body — which Christianity affirms as sacred and good
  • It is similar to the Filipino concept "Sapagkat Ako ay Tao Lamang" — using human weakness as an excuse for moral failure
  • It separates the spiritual from the physical, creating a fractured self
  • It leads to either contempt of the body (extreme asceticism) or license for the body (if the body doesn't matter spiritually)
Christian Understanding: The human person is a unity of body and soul, not two separate beings attached together. Our body is sacred because it is a precious gift from God and must be respected at all cost.
What are the Dogmas that Challenge Dualism?

The Incarnation

"The Word made flesh" (Jn 1:14). God became a human body — directly challenging the dualist claim that the body is inferior or evil.

The Resurrection of the Body

Christianity affirms that the whole person — body and soul — will be glorified. We do not shed the body at death; we are raised bodily.

The Sacraments as Material Signs of Grace

Grace is experienced through the body, not despite it — water, bread, wine, oil, touch. The material world is a vehicle of the sacred.

I appeal to you therefore, brothers and sisters, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship.

— Romans 12:1-2
The Christian Concept of Man — What are the key characteristics?
1

Image of God (Imago Dei)

Created in God's image (Gen 1:26). Our origin in God gives us intrinsic worth and moral responsibility. This dignity cannot be lost — though it can be distorted by sin.

2

Open and Relational

We are created for communion. Morality is not solitary — it is lived by being by others (conception), with others (family/friends), and for others (service). No one is an island.

3

Conscious Beings

Self-awareness through knowing and willing. Freedom as the foundation of morality. Consciousness enables us to choose the good intentionally. (cf. Gaudium et Spes 14)

4

Embodied Spirits

Unity of body and soul. The body is essential to human identity — good and honorable (GS 14). The Incarnation dignifies the body (Jn 1:14). Our physical actions are spiritual expressions of our dignity.

5

Historical

We are beings in process — shaped by our past, growing toward a future destiny. Morality involves constant growth and discerning God's will. We form ourselves through choices and are capable of conversion.

6

Unique Yet Fundamentally Equal

Equal in dignity despite differences — same origin, nature, and destiny (GS 29). Redeemed by Christ equally. This demands a morality of universal respect regardless of race, status, or achievement.

7

God's Partner

Called to divine communion. Christian morality is teleological — oriented toward destiny. We are called children of God, destined for eternal communion with the Trinity (Catechism 1692).

What is the Principle of Stewardship?
Pope Pius XII: "Man, in truth, is not the owner of his body nor its absolute lord, but only its user."

The human body ultimately belongs to God. We are stewards, not owners. This means we must care for the body but cannot do with it whatever we please. The principle is grounded in:

  • The dignity of the human body
  • The pre-eminence of the soul over the body
  • The solidarity of all peoples
  • The sovereign dominion of God over life and destiny
What is the Principle of Totality?

Pope Pius XII: A human person may allow individual parts of the body to be destroyed or mutilated when and to the extent that it is necessary for the good of the whole human organism.

Anatomical Mutilation

Removal of a bodily organ that changes human anatomy — e.g., mastectomy (cancerous breast removal), amputation of a gangrenous limb, surgical removal of a kidney.

Functional Mutilation

Suppression of the function of a bodily organ — e.g., sterilization. More morally complex; must meet the three conditions.

Three Conditions Required:

  1. The continued presence or functioning of a particular organ is causing serious damage or constitutes a threat to the organism
  2. The damage can be remedied or lessened by the mutilation being considered
  3. The negative effect (mutilation) is compensated for by the positive effect (elimination of danger of death or easing of pain)
Losing a leg is tragic. But losing a leg to save a life is morally justified.
What is the Principle of Double Effect?

A moral principle in Catholic moral theology (classically articulated by St. Thomas Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae) that explains when it is permissible to perform an action that has both a good effect and a bad effect.

Four Conditions (all must be met):

1

The Act Itself Must Be Good or Neutral

The action must not be intrinsically evil in its object.

2

The Good Effect Must Not Come From the Evil Effect

The good result cannot be achieved through the bad result — the evil cannot be the means to the good.

3

The Intention Must Be the Good Effect

The evil effect is tolerated (foreseen), not intended. The agent must intend only the good.

4

There Must Be Proportionate Reason

The good achieved must outweigh or be proportionate to the evil permitted.

Classic Example: A pregnant woman with uterine cancer undergoes a hysterectomy to remove the cancer (good effect = saving her life). The fetus dies as an unintended side effect (evil effect). The four conditions are met — this is morally permissible under Catholic teaching.
What is the Principle of Integral Cause (Totality of the Act)?
"For an act to be morally good, all essential elements of the act must be good. If even one essential element is morally defective, the whole act becomes morally wrong."

The three essentials of a morally good act:

Object (Act)

What you are doing — the nature of the action itself. Some acts are intrinsically evil regardless of intention.

Intention (Purpose)

Why you are doing it — the goal or motive. A good intention does not make an evil act good.

Circumstances

The situation, consequences, and context. Circumstances can increase or decrease moral goodness/evil but cannot reverse an intrinsically evil act.
This is also known as the "Fontes Moralitatis" (Sources of Morality) of an act. Catholic moral theology holds: "Bonum ex integra causa, malum ex quocumque defectu" — Good comes from a wholly good cause; evil comes from any single defect.

The Moral Law
The Objective Norm of Morality — Lesson 7
▼
What is Law? (St. Thomas Aquinas)

"Law is an ordinance of reason, promulgated by a competent authority, for the sake of the common good."

— St. Thomas Aquinas

Ordinance of Reason

Laws are rational deliberations intended to guide people toward what is good for them and for society.

Promulgated

Laws must be made known to those bound to observe them. The public must be aware of what is expected.

Legitimate Authority

Laws can only be valid if they are the legitimate exercise of authority by one who has charge of society.

Necessary to Man

Laws regulate human activity. Without them, even the best intentions are thwarted; without law, there is anarchy and chaos.
What is the Objective Moral Law and why is it important? (CCC 1950, 1954–1956)
CCC 1950: "Moral law is a work of divine wisdom." It prescribes ways and acts leading to man's goodness, and proscribes ways of evil that turn man away from God.
CCC 1954–1956: "The natural law expresses the original moral sense which enables man to discern by reason the good and the evil."

Moral relativism says morality depends only on culture, feelings, or situation. Catholic teaching affirms that some moral norms are objectively true because they reflect God's will and human nature.

Relevance / Importance of Moral Norms:

  1. Provide the objective criteria for conscience to judge what is morally good or evil.
  2. Help our moral development, especially in the formation of conscience.
  3. Offer the needed moral stability in our lives.
  4. Challenge us to stretch for an ideal beyond our limited experience, and correct our moral misconceptions in the process.
What are the Kinds of Law and their examples?

Divine Positive Law

Promulgated by the special command of God. Directs man towards his proper end.

Example: The Decalogue of Moses (Ten Commandments).

Human Positive Law

Promulgated by a legitimate human authority. Intended to preserve peace and harmony within society.

Examples: Code of Canon Law, Code of Civil Laws.

Both Divine Positive Law and Human Positive Law originate from Eternal Law — God's design as Supreme Creator to direct all created things to their proper end.

What are the three branches of Human Positive Law?
👨‍👩‍👧 Parental Authority ⛪ The Catholic Church 🏛️ The State
What are the Properties of Human Laws?
  1. Human laws must conform with the divine laws.
  2. Human laws must promote the common good — the sum total of social conditions which allow people to reach their fulfilment more fully and more easily.
  3. Human laws must be just and not discriminatory.
  4. Human laws must be practicable.
  5. Human laws regulate external actions only.
  6. Human laws are fallible because human legislators are liable to commit error.
What are the Expressions of Moral Law?
1

Eternal Law

The plan of God in creating the universe and in assigning to each creature their specific nature. It is unchanging Truth — God Himself. The work of divine wisdom.

2

Natural Law

The eternal law as knowable by sound human reason without the aid of supernatural revelation. Natural law is the human participation in God's wisdom. Basic precept: "Do good, avoid evil." It is the moral code that applies to all humans, knowable by reflecting rationally on our nature and purpose.

3

Revealed (Divine) Law

The way God has revealed Truth to us in the Scriptures and especially in Jesus Christ. It directs us toward our proper end. Example: The Ten Commandments.

4

Human Law

Drawn from Divine and Natural Law as "an ordinance of reason for the common good." Human laws are man-made and enforced by a ruler or government. Subdivided into: Civil law (law of the land) and Canon law (law of the Church).

What are the Properties of Natural Law?

Universal

Consistent throughout the world. It applies to each and every creature regardless of creed, race, culture, or historical circumstance.

Unchangeable

Consistent throughout time and setting, never changing. Morality written in man's heart.

Obligatory & Indispensable

Required by moral or legal rule; compulsory. Cannot be set aside or ignored.

Recognizable

Naturally knowable by all human beings through the use of reason alone.
The five inclinations of human nature that ground Natural Law: (1) inclination to the good, (2) to self-preservation, (3) to sexual union & rearing of offspring, (4) to knowledge of the Truth, (5) to live in society.

Conscience
The Judgment & Formation of Conscience
▼
What is Conscience?

The word conscience comes from the Latin con-scire — meaning "to know together" or "to know within oneself."

Conscience is the most secret core and sanctuary of the human person. There, people are alone with God, whose voice echoes in their depths.

— Gaudium et Spes

The Greeks were the first to reflect philosophically on the nature of conscience, understanding it as self-consciousness in its role of moral judgment — involving the influence of feeling and will.

"There is a higher court than courts of justice and that is the court of conscience. It supersedes all other courts." — Mohandas Gandhi
How is Conscience defined?
  • As a practical moral judgment (synderesis): the "dictate of conscience" — a judgment of reason that enjoins a person to do good and to avoid evil at the appropriate moment.
  • As a moral faculty (Augustine): the inner voice of God; the faculty which makes known to man his moral obligations and urges him to fulfill them.

🎁 Gift

Conscience is a gift — the God-given ability to discern right from wrong.

📋 Responsibility

Conscience is also a responsibility — we must form it rightly and follow it faithfully.

Two Basic Elements of Conscience:

Moral Judgment — discerns right from wrong Moral Obligation — commands to do good & avoid evil
What is Conscience in the Old Testament?

The word "conscience" appears only once in the Old Testament (Wisdom 17:11). In the OT it is understood as the internal voice of God, which continually calls for fidelity to the covenant. The teaching emphasizes what lies in the heart rather than external action, interior disposition, and the need for purity of intention.

What is Conscience in the New Testament?

The term is not found in the Gospels directly, but Jesus' teaching gives emphasis to the primacy, dignity, and inviolability of the moral conscience. The NT stresses:

  • What lies in the heart rather than external action.
  • Interior disposition as the source of moral life.
  • The need for purity of intention in all acts.
What are the Kinds of Conscience?

I. According to the Process of Time

A

Antecedent

Before the action. Helps sort out data and examine the morality of an act before performing it. It permits or forbids.

B

Concomitant

During the action. Actual awareness of being morally responsible for the goodness or badness of an act while doing it. It may scold (in case of habitual sins) or approve (in case of good habits).

C

Consequent

After the action. The process of looking back to review and evaluate the morality of what we have done. It approves or accuses.

II. In Relation to Certitude

Certain

Passes judgment without fear of error. Moral actions arise from sincere intention to do what is right (though it can still be objectively erroneous).

Doubtful

Uncertain about the morality of an action; suspends judgment or passes it with reasonable fear of erring.

III. According to the Objective Norm

TypeDescription
Right / CorrectAgrees with the objective norm (natural law).
ErroneousMistaken; does not agree with the objective norm.
Invincibly ErroneousError not due to one's own fault — no awareness of the error.
Vincibly ErroneousError due to one's own fault; happens when one is doubtful but takes no effort to find the truth.
PerplexedFears sin in whatever choice is made (conflict of values). Occurs in those less acquainted with moral norms.
LaxInclined to judge a thing allowable when objectively it is not, or considers a grave sin to be only light.
ScrupulousIn constant dread of sin where there is none, or of grave sin when there is only venial sin.
CompensatoryPreoccupied with small details of morality while concealing a fundamental lack of generosity in spiritual life.
When is conscience corrupted?

Like any of God's good gifts, conscience can be corrupted. This happens when we become accountable to no one but ourselves — the trap of moral relativism. A conscience is corrupted when it adopts a false notion of autonomy and rejects any objective moral standard outside itself.

How do we form our conscience?
  1. Conscience must be informed and enlightened.
  2. Education of conscience — prudent education teaches virtue; it prevents and cures fear, selfishness, pride, resentment from guilt, and complacency from weakness.
  3. Through the Word of God — interpret experience and the signs of the times assisted by the virtue of prudence, the advice of competent people, and the help of the Holy Spirit.
Aquinas argued that conscience should be educated to act toward real goods from God which encourage human flourishing — rather than the apparent goods of sensory pleasures alone.
How does one have an erroneous conscience?
  • Ignorance — a blinded conscience that lacks proper formation.
  • Bad example — corrupting influences from others in one's environment.
  • Enslavement to passions — allowing emotions or desires to override reason.
  • Mistaken notion of autonomy — believing conscience is answerable to no one.
  • Rejection of the Church's authority and teaching.
  • Lack of conversion and charity.

Sin
The Nature of Sin — Lesson 9
▼
What is the Definition of Sin? (CCC 1849–1850)
  • An offense against reason, truth, and right conscience.
  • A failure in genuine love for God and neighbor.
  • It wounds the nature of the human person and injures human solidarity.
"All moral reality is made up of the objective nature of the act, the intention of the agent, and the circumstances."

Sin as a moral reality is an attitude, an action or failure to act, or a power/force that leads us into evil.

What is Sin as a Moral Reality — Sin as Alienation? (CFC 1083)

Sin separates us from — alienates us from, i.e., makes us strangers from — our true selves (intra-personal), our neighbors (inter-personal), the larger community (societal), and God, the ground and source of all three.

— CFC, 1083
What is Sin as Deviation and Contradiction?

Sin as Deviation

A voluntary turning away or departure from the right order — a free choice to step off the path of the good.

Sin as Contradiction

An internal conflict between the true nature of man as oriented to God and his earthly pre-occupations and tendencies. Sin is also called sin as failure to love.
What are some erroneous notions of sin?
❌ Error 1
Some people think sin is just breaking a rule in a book.
Correction: Sin is a relational rupture — it wounds one's relationship with God, self, and others, not merely a violation of an abstract code.
❌ Error 2
Others think sin is only a feeling of guilt.
Correction: Sin is an objective moral reality independent of one's emotional state. One can sin without feeling guilty, and feel guilty without having sinned.
❌ Error 3
Some believe sin cannot be avoided.
Correction: While humans are prone to sin through concupiscence, they retain freedom and responsibility. God's grace makes avoidance of sin possible.
❌ Error 4
The idea that whatever a person does, the consequences affect even the grandchildren in an absolute, fatalistic way.
Correction: While social sin has lasting effects, each person is responsible for their own choices. Conversion and grace can break cycles of sin.
What is the basic notion of Sin in the Old Testament?

In the OT, sin is often looked upon as a transgression of God's law and will. The sin of Adam and Eve is not only the "original" but also the typical infidelity of humankind.

Three Basic Notions of Sin in the OT (CFC #766)

  • Missing the mark
  • Depravity and perversity
  • Rebellion and transgression
What is the Concept of Sin in the Old Testament?
OT ConceptMeaning
TransgressionDeliberate violation of God's law and will.
InfidelityUnfaithfulness to the covenant relationship with God.
Rupture of the CovenantSin breaks the spousal bond between God and His people; it is a rupture of the covenant relationship.
Negative ResponseA refusal of God's call; it leads to "death," both spiritual and physical.
What is the Concept of Sin in the New Testament?

Ungrateful Desertion

Sin is considered as ungrateful desertion of the Lord, illustrated by the Prodigal Son (Lk. 15:11–32).

Enmity Against God

The sinner lives in enmity against God; sinners are excluded from the Kingdom of God (1 Cor. 6:9; Gal. 5:21). The capital sin of the NT is the refusal to recognize the power of God in Christ (Jn. 8:24).
What are the Capital Sins?

Listed by Gregory the Great (604). Called "capital" not because they are always necessarily grave, but because they easily become vices and sources of many other sins.

Pride / Vainglory

Inordinate desire for honor and independence. Opposed to the virtue of humility.

Avarice / Greed

Inordinate pursuit of material goods; desiring what belongs to others, leading to dishonesty and injustice.

Envy

Discontent over the good of one's neighbor, considered detrimental to oneself. Opposed to brotherliness.

Lust

Inordinate craving for sexual gratification. Opposed to the virtue of chastity.

Gluttony

Excessive enjoyment of food and drink. Opposed to temperance and sobriety.

Anger / Wrath

Intemperate outburst of dislike with inordinate desire for another's punishment. Opposed to patience.

Sloth

Laziness; in a narrow sense, religious/spiritual sloth — a turning away from spiritual things because of the effort they require.
Memory Aid: PEGSLAW — Pride, Envy, Gluttony, Sloth, Lust, Avarice/Greed, Wrath/Anger
What are the Dimensions of Sin?

Personal Dimension

Sin that destroys one's inner wholeness — it is intra-personal, harming the sinner's relationship with themselves.

Social Dimension

Sin that affects and causes harm to one's neighbor — it is inter-personal, damaging relationships in community.

Religious Dimension

Sin that rejects and ignores the structures established by God — ultimately a rejection of God Himself.
What are the Degrees of Sin?

Grave / Mortal Sin

From the Latin mors (death). A morally wrong decision so intensive it gives a wrong orientation to man's life and causes a total break in our relationship with God.

Venial Sin

From the Latin venia (pardon). A sin that causes a weakening or a strain in our relationship with God, but not a total rupture.
What are the 3 Conditions of Grave Sin? (CCC 1858–1859)
1

Full Knowledge

There is full knowledge or full awareness that the choice being made is wrong.

2

Gravity of Matter

The gravity or seriousness of the matter — not every wrong act rises to the level of grave sin.

3

Full Consent

There is full consent of the free will that presupposes the actual doing of the wrong option — not merely temptation but deliberate choice.

What are the Kinds of Internal Sins?

Internal sins occur in the mind — in thought and desire:

  1. Mental Complacency in Sinful Imaginations — takes pleasure in sinful thoughts or desires without intending to act on them.
  2. Sinful Joy — takes pleasure in an evil deed done to another.
  3. Sinful Regret — disappointment in not having performed an evil deed.
  4. Evil Desire — the desire or resolution to perform a sinful action.
  5. Prejudice or Bias — a preconceived judgment that causes injury to another person's rights.

Moral Actions
Object, End, and Circumstances
▼
What is the difference between Human Acts and Acts of Man?
Human ActsActs of Man
Actions performed with knowledge, freedom, and voluntariness.Actions done without full deliberation or consent — reflexes, actions under compulsion, or while asleep/unconscious.
Subject to moral evaluation — can be called good or evil.Not subject to full moral evaluation; the person is not fully responsible.
Example: Deliberately lying to someone.Example: Sneezing; sleepwalking; an involuntary reflex.
What are the Elements of Human Acts?
1

Knowledge

A person is not responsible for an act done in ignorance, unless the ignorance is the person's own fault and is therefore willed. Knowledge is prerequisite to full moral responsibility.

2

Freedom

"Freedom makes man responsible for his acts to the extent that they are voluntary." (CCC 1734). Without freedom, there is no moral culpability.

3

Voluntariness

From the Latin voluntas (will). Voluntariness is essential to a human act — it is the act of the will directing toward a chosen end.

What are the Determinants of Morality?

1. Object (Act Itself)

The object is the act of the will — the primary source for the judgment of an action. A lie remains a lie regardless of purpose. Some acts are intrinsically evil; others are intrinsically good; some are indifferent and take their morality from intention and circumstance.

2. Circumstances

Conditions outside the act that influence it by increasing or lessening voluntariness/freedom, thus affecting morality. They can make a good act better or a bad act worse — but cannot make an intrinsically evil act good.

3. Intention (End)

The reason or objective for which the agent undertakes the act. The movement of the will toward the end. An essential element of moral evaluation.
Catholic moral theology holds: "Bonum ex integra causa, malum ex quocumque defectu" — Good comes from a wholly good cause; evil comes from any single defect. All three determinants must be good for the act to be morally good.
What are the different types of Circumstances?
CircumstanceQuestionExample
PersonWHO? — the doer (agent) of the act and the receiver or person to whom the act is done.Giving aid to orphans is good; if done by a Metro Aide (who is poor himself), the good act becomes more meritorious than if done by a big-time businessman who earns millions.
PlaceWHERE? — the particular space where the act is performed.Creating scandal inside a church is worse than outside it.
TimeWHEN? — the moment when the act is performed.Fasting on Ash Wednesday is a good act made better by its timing.
MannerHOW? — the way the agent manages to do the act.Seducing someone using a false promise of marriage aggravates the act.
Condition of AgentWHY / WHAT CONDITION? — ignorance, fear, habits, emotions.Missing Sunday Mass due to invincible ignorance removes the sin.
Thing ItselfWHAT? — the special quality of the object involved.Stealing ₱1M is worse than stealing ₱7; stealing a famous relic is worse than stealing a ballpen.
MeansBY WHAT MEANS / WHOSE HELP?Robbing a bank with the help of the bank's own security personnel (inside job) is aggravated by this circumstance.
What are the Principles of Intention?
1

Good + Good = Doubly Good

An act that is good in itself and done for a good end becomes doubly good. Ex: Giving money to the poor to alleviate their suffering.

2

Bad + Bad = Doubly Bad

An act that is bad in itself and done with a bad end becomes doubly bad. Ex: Accepting a bribe to continue vices.

3

Good + Bad Intention = Bad

An act that is good in itself but done with a bad intention becomes bad. Ex: A governor visiting flood victims only for media coverage.

4

Bad + Good Intention = Still Bad

An act that is bad in itself and done with a good end does not become good (though guilt may be lessened). Ex: Cheating on an exam to save a scholarship.

5

Indifferent + Good Intention = Good

An indifferent act done for a good end becomes good. Ex: Walking into a store to help a stranger.

6

Indifferent + Bad Intention = Bad

An indifferent act done for a bad end becomes bad. Ex: Walking into a store to steal.
